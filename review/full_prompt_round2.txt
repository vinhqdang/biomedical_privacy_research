\begin{abstract}
\noindent \textbf{Background:} The clinical deployment of deep learning systems is impeded by four interconnected requirements: patient data privacy, algorithmic fairness across demographic cohorts, reliable uncertainty calibration, and faithful explainability. When applied naively, these dimensions exhibit destructive interference—forming the \emph{Privacy--Fairness--Uncertainty Trilemma}, where standard Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) disproportionately suppresses gradient signals from underrepresented patient groups, impairs calibration, and yields degraded feature attributions.

\noindent \textbf{Methods:} We propose \textbf{TRACER-Med}, a principled trustworthy deep learning architecture that harmonizes these objectives. TRACER-Med integrates four synergistic mechanisms: (i) an \emph{Evidential Dirichlet Deep Learning} formulation parameterizing second-order beliefs to separate aleatoric data entropy from epistemic clinical vacuity; (ii) a \emph{Subgroup-Calibrated DP Optimizer} (SC-DP-SGD) that enforces group-conditioned clipping thresholds and dynamic sensitivity reweighting calibrated to global $C_{\max}$ under exact R\'enyi Differential Privacy; (iii) a \emph{Differentially Private Group-Conditional Conformal Predictor} (DP-GCCP) establishing finite-sample, distribution-free coverage across protected demographics with strict privacy budget composition ($\epsilon_{\text{total}} = \epsilon_{\text{model}} + \epsilon_{\text{conf}}$); and (iv) a \emph{Dual-Channel Dirichlet Attribution} (DCDA) mechanism providing path-integrated feature attributions that decouple features driving diagnostic disease evidence from those generating epistemic clinical uncertainty.

\noindent \textbf{Results:} Comprehensive evaluations across three standardized biomedical benchmarks—Statlog Heart Disease ($N=270$), Pima Indians Diabetes ($N=768$), and a Simulated Inpatient EHR Cohort ($N=10{,}000$, parameterized from MIMIC-IV and eICU distributions)—demonstrate that TRACER-Med mitigates up to 74\% of DP-induced fairness disparity while maintaining competitive diagnostic discrimination (ROC-AUC $0.85\pm0.02$). It compresses subgroup conformal coverage gaps to $\le 3.1\%$ at nominal 90\% coverage, maintains empirical privacy against membership inference attacks (MIA AUC $\approx 0.52$), and yields faithful dual-channel explanations.

\noindent \textbf{Conclusions:} TRACER-Med provides a mathematically grounded, clinically actionable blueprint for deploying safe, fair, and privacy-compliant artificial intelligence in high-stakes healthcare settings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Artificial intelligence and deep neural networks have demonstrated diagnostic accuracy comparable to domain specialists across medical specialties, including acute mortality triage, cardiovascular risk scoring, digital pathology, and medical imaging~\cite{topol2019high,kelly2019key}. Despite these achievements, translations into bedside clinical workflows remain limited. Clinical oversight committees, practitioners, and international regulatory authorities (including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, HHS Office for Civil Rights, and European Union AI Act) recognize that predictive discrimination alone is insufficient for clinical adoption. Safe deployment requires adherence to four cardinal pillars of \emph{Trustworthy Medical AI}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Provable Patient Privacy:} Models trained on confidential Electronic Health Records (EHRs) and patient diagnostic registries must provide rigorous bounds against re-identification and attribute reconstruction attacks, formalized via Differential Privacy (DP)~\cite{abadi2016deep,mironov2017renyi}.
    \item \textbf{Subgroup Algorithmic Fairness:} Prognostic and diagnostic models must not exacerbate healthcare disparities across demographic cohorts defined by biological sex, age, race, or socioeconomic indicators~\cite{obermeyer2019dissecting,rajkomar2018ensuring,seyyed2021underdiagnosis}.
    \item \textbf{Calibrated Uncertainty Quantification:} High-stakes medicine requires systems capable of expressing their own limitations. Algorithms must output well-calibrated probabilities, distinguish irreducible observational noise (aleatoric uncertainty) from missing diagnostic evidence (epistemic vacuity), and provide distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantees~\cite{kompa2021second,sensoy2018evidential,angelopoulos2021gentle}.
    \item \textbf{Faithful and Actionable Explainability:} Clinicians cannot safely act on uninterpretable decisions. Models must provide transparent feature attributions that explain not only why an alert was triggered, but also which physiological parameters are driving clinical hesitation~\cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic,lundberg2017unified}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Privacy--Fairness--Uncertainty Trilemma in Healthcare}
Historically, these four dimensions have been investigated in separate subfields. When naively combined in deep neural networks, they exhibit significant friction, giving rise to what we characterize as the \emph{Privacy--Fairness--Uncertainty Trilemma}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Differential Privacy Disproportionately Impacts Minority Accuracy:} In standard Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD)~\cite{abadi2016deep}, uniform per-sample gradient clipping and isotropic Gaussian noise addition produce an asymmetric equity penalty. Because underrepresented patient groups often exhibit distinct physiological presentations, their gradient norms and directions differ from the majority cohort. Uniform clipping disproportionately truncates minority gradients, while the shared noise floor degrades the effective Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) of minority updates, widening diagnostic performance gaps~\cite{bagdasaryan2019differential,suriyakumar2021chasing,tran2021differentially,xu2021removing}.
    \item \textbf{DP Perturbations Induce Subgroup Miscalibration:} Softmax-parameterized neural networks trained with DP-SGD often suffer from substantial probability distortion and miscalibration. Furthermore, this calibration degradation is unevenly distributed across demographic cohorts, leading to biased risk stratification in clinical triage~\cite{farrand2020neither,xu2023fair}.
    \item \textbf{Standard Conformal Prediction Fails Conditional Subgroup Guarantees:} While split conformal prediction guarantees marginal coverage $1 - \alpha_{\text{conf}}$ over the aggregate population~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2021gentle}, it does not guarantee subgroup-conditional validity. When conformal quantiles are estimated under differential privacy constraints, minority cohorts frequently experience coverage deficits, receiving prediction sets that fail to cover the true clinical condition at the nominal safety level~\cite{romano2019conformalized,gibbs2023conformal}.
    \item \textbf{Gradient Perturbations Degrade Feature Attributions:} Post-hoc gradient attribution techniques (e.g., Integrated Gradients) depend on model input gradients. Under differential privacy, network weight constraints and regularization induce significant shifts in decision boundaries, often resulting in noisy, unstable attributions that fail faithfulness evaluations and obscure the true clinical rationale.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Our Contributions}
To address these challenges within a cohesive architectural design, we propose \textbf{TRACER-Med} (\textbf{TR}ustworthy, \textbf{A}ccredited, \textbf{C}onformalized, \textbf{E}vidential, and Differentially P\textbf{R}ivate Deep Learning for \textbf{Med}icine). Our primary contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic Dissection of Inter-Pillar Trade-offs:} We provide an analytical and empirical formulation of the Privacy--Fairness--Uncertainty Trilemma in clinical machine learning, demonstrating why isolated privacy, fairness, and uncertainty interventions fail when deployed on demographically imbalanced patient registries.
    \item \textbf{Evidential Dirichlet Deep Learning Formulation:} We formulate a second-order Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) backbone that maps clinical patient representations to Dirichlet concentration parameters. This analytically separates aleatoric entropy from epistemic vacuity, providing calibrated uncertainty estimates under private optimization.
    \item \textbf{Subgroup-Calibrated DP Optimization (SC-DP-SGD):} We derive an adaptive, group-conditioned clipping and dynamic sensitivity reweighting mechanism that equalizes gradient signal-to-noise ratios across protected demographic groups. In Theorem~\ref{thm:privacy}, we prove that by calibrating noise to the worst-case sensitivity $C_{\max}$ using fixed population demographic priors, SC-DP-SGD provides unconditional, rigorous R\'enyi Differential Privacy guarantees while reducing DP-induced fairness disparity by up to 74\%.
    \item \textbf{Differentially Private Group-Conditional Conformal Prediction (DP-GCCP):} We develop a private conformal calibration framework incorporating group-conditional non-conformity partitions, finite-sample concentration adjustments, and private quantile estimation. We formalize the privacy composition between model training and conformal calibration ($\epsilon_{\text{total}} = \epsilon_{\text{model}} + \epsilon_{\text{conf}}$).
    \item \textbf{Dual-Channel Dirichlet Attribution (DCDA):} Leveraging the closed-form structure of Dirichlet evidence, we introduce a dual-channel path-integrated attribution formulation. Channel A attributes input features driving positive diagnostic risk, while Channel B isolates features driving epistemic uncertainty and clinical doubt.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Empirical Validation:} We evaluate TRACER-Med across three clinical benchmarks: Statlog Heart Disease ($N=270$), Pima Indians Diabetes ($N=768$), and a simulated 10,000-patient Inpatient EHR Cohort (parameterized from MIMIC-IV and eICU distributions). We demonstrate Pareto-superior balance across clinical utility (ROC-AUC), calibration (ECE), demographic parity, equalized odds, empirical membership inference resistance (MIA AUC), conformal coverage equity, and attribution fidelity.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Differential Privacy in Biomedical Machine Learning}
Differential Privacy (DP)~\cite{abadi2016deep} provides a formal framework for bounding the privacy risk of individual patient records during model training. In deep learning, DP-SGD restricts per-sample gradient $\ell_2$ norms by clipping to a threshold $C$, adds Gaussian perturbation $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 \mathbf{I})$, and accumulates privacy loss via R\'enyi Differential Privacy (RDP)~\cite{mironov2017renyi,mironov2019renyi} or Gaussian Differential Privacy (GDP)~\cite{dong2022gaussian}.

However, Bagdasaryan et al.~\cite{bagdasaryan2019differential} and Suriyakumar et al.~\cite{suriyakumar2021chasing} demonstrated that standard DP-SGD acts as an equity penalty: gradient clipping truncates underrepresented patient examples more severely, widening demographic error gaps. Recent algorithmic proposals have explored fair DP in generic classification settings, such as adaptive clipping (Tran et al.~\cite{tran2021differentially}, Xu et al.~\cite{xu2021removing}) and gradient angle alignment (Esipova et al.~\cite{esipova2022gradient}). Nevertheless, integrating fair DP optimization with formal evidential uncertainty quantification and distribution-free conformal calibration remains an open challenge in biomedical informatics.

\subsection{Algorithmic Fairness in Clinical Healthcare}
Algorithmic bias in biomedical models is well-documented. Obermeyer et al.~\cite{obermeyer2019dissecting} showed that commercial risk algorithms systematically prioritized healthier White patients over sicker Black patients for high-risk care management programs. Seyyed-Kalantari et al.~\cite{seyyed2021underdiagnosis} identified that convolutional networks for chest radiographs exhibited substantial underdiagnosis bias in female and underserved populations. Typical fairness interventions comprise pre-processing, in-processing (e.g., representation debiasing via optimal transport and Sliced Wasserstein Distance~\cite{kolouri2019generalized}), and post-processing (e.g., equalized odds thresholding~\cite{hardt2016equality}). However, the interaction of these interventions with differentially private gradient sanitization requires careful design to prevent noise from neutralizing fairness gains.

\subsection{Evidential Uncertainty Quantification and Conformal Prediction}
Standard deep neural networks with softmax parameterizations tend to output overconfident probabilities on out-of-distribution (OOD) or ambiguous clinical profiles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple}. Evidential Deep Learning (EDL)~\cite{sensoy2018evidential} parameterizes a Dirichlet distribution over categorical probability vectors, allowing analytical decomposition into aleatoric entropy and epistemic vacuity. While recent studies have examined theoretical limitations of loss-minimized evidential learning~\cite{bengs2022pitfalls}, evidential parameterizations offer natural closed-form analytical properties suitable for path-integrated gradient explanations.

Conformal Prediction (CP)~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2021gentle} constructs distribution-free, finite-sample prediction sets $\mathcal{C}(X)$ that satisfy a marginal coverage guarantee $\mathbb{P}(Y \in \mathcal{C}(X)) \ge 1 - \alpha_{\text{conf}}$. To enforce equity across demographic subgroups, Mondrian and group-conditional conformal prediction~\cite{romano2019conformalized,gibbs2023conformal} compute group-specific non-conformity quantiles. Extending conformal prediction to differentially private settings requires private quantile mechanisms~\cite{angelopoulos2021private} to avoid membership leakage while preserving bounded set size efficiency.

\subsection{Explainable AI and Attribution Robustness}
Attribution methods such as Integrated Gradients (IG)~\cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic} provide axiomatic guarantees (including completeness and implementation invariance) for feature attribution. Most existing attribution frameworks are single-channel, explaining only the predicted disease class. However, in clinical decision support, identifying features that contribute to epistemic uncertainty is equally essential~\cite{antoran2021clue,perez2022uncertainty}. Furthermore, assessing explanation fidelity (e.g., via Infidelity metrics~\cite{yeh2019infidelity}) under differential privacy constraints is crucial to ensure that clinicians receive reliable diagnostic explanations.

\section{Problem Formulation and Notation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Formal Setting and Notation}
Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i, a_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ denote a clinical dataset where $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ represents patient clinical features, $y_i \in \{0, 1, \dots, K-1\}$ denotes the clinical diagnosis or risk outcome, and $a_i \in \mathcal{A} = \{0, 1\}$ represents a binary protected demographic attribute. Let $p_a = \mathbb{P}(A = a)$ denote the fixed population prior prevalence of group $a$, and let $n_a = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \mathbb{I}(a_i = a)$ denote the count of group $a$ within a mini-batch $\mathcal{B} \subset \mathcal{D}$.

\noindent \textbf{Differential Privacy:} A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\epsilon, \delta)$-Differential Privacy~\cite{abadi2016deep} if for all neighboring datasets $\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{D}'$ differing by at most one patient record, and for all measurable sets of outputs $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \text{Range}(\mathcal{M})$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}) \in \mathcal{S}) \le e^\epsilon \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}') \in \mathcal{S}) + \delta
\end{equation}
Under R\'enyi Differential Privacy (RDP) of order $\lambda_{\text{RDP}} > 1$~\cite{mironov2017renyi}, the R\'enyi divergence of order $\lambda_{\text{RDP}}$ between $\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})$ and $\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}')$ satisfies $D_{\lambda_{\text{RDP}}}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}) \parallel \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}')) \le \epsilon(\lambda_{\text{RDP}})$.

\noindent \textbf{Algorithmic Fairness Criteria:}
We evaluate two standard clinical equity metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Demographic Parity Disparity ($\Delta_{\text{DP}}$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta_{\text{DP}} = |\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0) - \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)|
    \end{equation}
    \item \emph{Equalized Odds Disparity ($\Delta_{\text{EO}}$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta_{\text{EO}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( |\text{FPR}_0 - \text{FPR}_1| + |\text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1| \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{FPR}_a$ and $\text{TPR}_a$ denote the False Positive Rate and True Positive Rate within demographic cohort $a \in \{0, 1\}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Identified Research Gaps}
Our analysis identifies four core technical gaps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gap 1 (Disparate Impact from Uniform Gradient Clipping):} In demographically imbalanced clinical cohorts ($p_0 < p_1$), minority patients often present distinct clinical presentations. Under standard DP-SGD with uniform clipping threshold $C$, gradients from minority patients are clipped more severely. When combined with isotropic noise $\sigma C$, the minority gradient update suffers from reduced Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), impairing minority performance~\cite{bagdasaryan2019differential,suriyakumar2021chasing}.
    \item \textbf{Gap 2 (Uncertainty Conflation under Differential Privacy):} Cross-entropy classifiers with softmax outputs output point estimates that conflate aleatoric data entropy (physiological variability) with epistemic vacuity (deficiency of clinical evidence). Under DP gradient noise, probability estimates miscalibrate, yielding severe Expected Calibration Error (ECE)~\cite{farrand2020neither}.
    \item \textbf{Gap 3 (Subgroup Coverage Deficits in Conformal Prediction):} Standard marginal conformal prediction guarantees aggregate population coverage $1 - \alpha_{\text{conf}}$, but permits substantial coverage gaps between demographic groups: $\mathbb{P}(Y \in \mathcal{C}(X) \mid A = 0) \ll 1 - \alpha_{\text{conf}}$. Estimating group quantiles privately without excessive set size inflation is essential for clinical equity~\cite{romano2019conformalized,angelopoulos2021private}.
    \item \textbf{Gap 4 (Single-Channel Explanations):} Existing feature attribution methods provide a single attribution score for predicted classes, failing to inform clinicians which physiological features are driving model uncertainty and clinical doubt~\cite{antoran2021clue}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evidential Dirichlet Deep Learning Formulation}
Rather than predicting a single categorical probability vector via softmax, TRACER-Med parameterizes a second-order Dirichlet distribution over multinomial probabilities. The network encoder and non-negative evidence head map patient features $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ to evidence vector $\mathbf{e} = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \text{softplus}(\mathbf{z}) \ge \mathbf{0}$, defining Dirichlet concentration parameters:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_k = e_k + 1, \quad S = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the total Dirichlet evidence strength. The expected categorical probability for class $k$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_k = \frac{\alpha_k}{S}
\end{equation}
Subjective Logic parameterizes the epistemic uncertainty (vacuity of evidence) as:
\begin{equation}
    u = \frac{K}{S} \in (0, 1]
\end{equation}
When the network observes novel or anomalous clinical features, $\mathbf{e} \to \mathbf{0}$, yielding $\alpha_k \to 1$, $S \to K$, and epistemic uncertainty $u \to 1$. Conversely, abundant clinical evidence drives $S \gg K$, yielding $u \to 0$. In parallel, aleatoric data uncertainty is captured by the Shannon entropy of the expected probabilities: $\mathcal{H}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}) = -\sum_{k=1}^K \hat{p}_k \ln \hat{p}_k$.

The Evidential loss combines the Bayesian expected cross-entropy loss under the Dirichlet distribution with a Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence regularizer that penalizes misleading evidence for non-target classes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{EDL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^K y_{ik} \left( \psi(S_i) - \psi(\alpha_{ik}) \right) + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\text{Dir}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_i) \parallel \text{Dir}(\mathbf{1})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
where $\psi(\cdot)$ denotes the digamma function, and $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_i = \mathbf{y}_i + (1 - \mathbf{y}_i) \odot \boldsymbol{\alpha}_i$.

\subsection{Subgroup-Calibrated Differentially Private Optimization (SC-DP-SGD)}
To prevent differential privacy from exacerbating demographic disparity, we introduce the \emph{Subgroup-Calibrated DP-SGD} (SC-DP-SGD) optimizer. 

\noindent \textbf{Population-Calibrated Weights:} To prevent batch demographic counts from introducing sensitivity variations, we compute dynamic scaling weights from fixed population priors $p_a = \mathbb{P}(A = a)$:
\begin{equation}
    w_a = \left( \frac{1}{2 p_a} \right)^\gamma, \quad \gamma \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}
normalized to satisfy $\mathbb{E}_{A \sim p}[w_A^2] = 1$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_a = \frac{w_a}{\sqrt{\sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}} p_b w_b^2}}
\end{equation}
For each patient example $i$ belonging to demographic group $a_i \in \{0, 1\}$, the per-sample gradient $g_i = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell_i$ is clipped using the group-conditioned bound $C_{a_i} = C \cdot \tilde{w}_{a_i}$:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{g}_i = g_i \cdot \min\left(1, \frac{C \cdot \tilde{w}_{a_i}}{\|g_i\|_2}\right)
\end{equation}
The maximum $\ell_2$ sensitivity across any single patient replacement is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
    C_{\max} = C \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \tilde{w}_a
\end{equation}
The aggregated gradient is sanitized by injecting Gaussian noise scaled to the worst-case sensitivity $C_{\max}$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{G} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \left( \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \bar{g}_i + \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 C_{\max}^2 \mathbf{I}\right) \right)
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Privacy Guarantee of SC-DP-SGD]
\label{thm:privacy}
Let $\mathcal{M}$ denote the SC-DP-SGD algorithm operating on dataset $\mathcal{D}$ with sample size $N$, Poisson subsampling ratio $q = |\mathcal{B}| / N$, total training steps $T$, base clipping bound $C$, group clipping thresholds $C_a = C \tilde{w}_a$, and noise standard deviation $\sigma C_{\max}$, where $C_{\max} = C \max_a \tilde{w}_a$. 

Then, $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\lambda_{\text{RDP}}, \epsilon(\lambda_{\text{RDP}}))$-R\'enyi Differential Privacy for any order $\lambda_{\text{RDP}} > 1$, with:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon(\lambda_{\text{RDP}}) \le \frac{q^2 \lambda_{\text{RDP}}}{2 \sigma^2} \cdot T
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies standard $(\epsilon, \delta)$-Differential Privacy for any target $\delta > 0$, with:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon = \min_{\lambda_{\text{RDP}} > 1} \left( \epsilon(\lambda_{\text{RDP}}) + \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\lambda_{\text{RDP}} - 1} \right)
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{D}'$ be neighboring datasets differing by at most one patient record $j$. In SC-DP-SGD, replacing record $j$ with an arbitrary alternative record $j'$ (belonging to any group $a \in \mathcal{A}$) alters at most one clipped gradient $\bar{g}_j$. Since $\|\bar{g}_j\|_2 \le C_{a_j} \le C_{\max}$, the global $\ell_2$ sensitivity of the batch sum $\Delta_2 = \sup_{\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{D}'} \|\sum_{i} \bar{g}_i(\mathcal{D}) - \sum_{i} \bar{g}_i(\mathcal{D}')\|_2 \le C_{\max}$. 

Because the injected Gaussian noise has covariance $\sigma^2 C_{\max}^2 \mathbf{I}$, the effective noise multiplier relative to the global $\ell_2$ sensitivity is $\frac{\sigma C_{\max}}{\Delta_2} \ge \frac{\sigma C_{\max}}{C_{\max}} = \sigma$. Applying the R\'enyi Differential Privacy analysis for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism~\cite{mironov2017renyi,mironov2019renyi}, each subsampled step satisfies RDP of order $\lambda_{\text{RDP}}$ with divergence at most $\frac{q^2 \lambda_{\text{RDP}}}{2 \sigma^2}$. By linear RDP composition over $T$ steps, the cumulative divergence satisfies $\epsilon(\lambda_{\text{RDP}}) \le \frac{q^2 \lambda_{\text{RDP}}}{2 \sigma^2} T$. Conversion to $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP follows directly from the standard RDP-to-DP conversion formula~\cite{mironov2017renyi}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Latent Sliced Wasserstein Demographic Alignment}
To regularize representation fairness during non-private training and feature extraction, TRACER-Med applies Sliced Wasserstein Distance (SWD)~\cite{kolouri2019generalized} alignment to the penultimate latent representations $\mathbf{z} = h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{fair}} = \mathcal{SW}(P(\mathbf{z} \mid A = 0), P(\mathbf{z} \mid A = 1)) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \int_0^1 \left| F_{0, \boldsymbol{\omega}_m}^{-1}(t) - F_{1, \boldsymbol{\omega}_m}^{-1}(t) \right| dt
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\omega}_m \sim \text{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})$ are random unit projection vectors, and $F_{a, \boldsymbol{\omega}_m}^{-1}$ represents the 1D quantile function of group $a$'s projected representations.

\subsection{Differentially Private Group-Conditional Conformal Prediction (DP-GCCP)}
Following model convergence, TRACER-Med calibrates conformal prediction sets on a held-out calibration split $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}} = \{(\mathbf{x}_j, y_j, a_j)\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{cal}}}$, disjoint from training data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$. Non-conformity scores are computed as the residual probability of the true label:
\begin{equation}
    s_j = 1 - \hat{p}_{y_j}(\mathbf{x}_j)
\end{equation}
To ensure distribution-free finite-sample coverage without leaking patient calibration scores, we partition calibration scores by demographic group: $\mathcal{S}_a = \{s_j : a_j = a\}$. For each group $a$, we compute a private conformal threshold $\hat{q}_a$ using the Laplace mechanism applied to the finite-sample adjusted quantile index $\lceil (|\mathcal{S}_a| + 1)(1 - \alpha_{\text{conf}} + \tau_a) \rceil / |\mathcal{S}_a|$ with privacy budget $\epsilon_{\text{conf}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{q}_a = \min\left(1.0, \, \text{Quantile}_{\lceil (|\mathcal{S}_a|+1)(1 - \alpha_{\text{conf}} + \tau_a) \rceil / |\mathcal{S}_a|}(\mathcal{S}_a) + \text{Lap}\left( \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_a| \cdot \epsilon_{\text{conf}}} \right)\right)
\end{equation}
where $\tau_a = \frac{2 \ln(10)}{|\mathcal{S}_a| \cdot \epsilon_{\text{conf}}}$ is a finite-sample concentration buffer guaranteeing that noise does not cause undercoverage with high probability~\cite{angelopoulos2021private}. By parallel composition over disjoint demographic groups and basic composition across splits, the total privacy cost of the pipeline is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\text{total}} = \epsilon_{\text{model}} + \epsilon_{\text{conf}}
\end{equation}
The prediction set for a query patient $\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}$ with protected attribute $a$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}) = \left\{ k \in \{0, \dots, K-1\} : 1 - \hat{p}_k(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}) \le \hat{q}_a \right\}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dual-Channel Dirichlet Attribution (DCDA)}
Traditional explainability frameworks generate a single attribution score for the predicted class. TRACER-Med leverages the analytical properties of Dirichlet evidence to compute dual-channel path-integrated feature attributions relative to a neutral baseline $\mathbf{x}^0$:
\begin{align}
    \Phi_j^{\text{diag}}(\mathbf{x}) &= (x_j - x_j^0) \int_0^1 \frac{\partial \ln \alpha_{\hat{y}}(\mathbf{x}^0 + t(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^0))}{\partial x_j} dt \quad \text{(Channel A: Diagnostic Evidence)} \label{eq:dcda_a} \\
    \Phi_j^{\text{unc}}(\mathbf{x}) &= (x_j - x_j^0) \int_0^1 \frac{\partial u(\mathbf{x}^0 + t(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^0))}{\partial x_j} dt \quad \text{(Channel B: Epistemic Uncertainty)} \label{eq:dcda_b}
\end{align}
where $\hat{y} = \arg\max_k \hat{p}_k(\mathbf{x})$ is the predicted class. Channel A provides diagnostic feature attribution explaining which biomarkers support disease presence, while Channel B pinpoints which physiological measurements are driving epistemic uncertainty.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{TRACER-Med Complete Training and Conformal Calibration Pipeline}
\label{alg:tracer}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Dataset $\mathcal{D}$, privacy budget $(\epsilon, \delta)$, clipping bound $C$, noise scale $\sigma$, conformal significance $\alpha_{\text{conf}}$, population priors $\{p_0, p_1\}$.
\ENSURE Private model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$, group conformal thresholds $\{\hat{q}_0, \hat{q}_1\}$.
\STATE Split $\mathcal{D}$ into Train $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$, Calibration $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$, and Test $\mathcal{D}_{\text{te}}$.
\STATE Initialize Evidential Network $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ with LayerNorm normalizations.
\STATE Compute population weights: $\tilde{w}_a = \frac{p_a^{-\gamma}}{\sqrt{\sum_b p_b p_b^{-2\gamma}}}$ and $C_{\max} = C \max_a \tilde{w}_a$.
\FOR{epoch $t = 1$ to $E$}
    \FOR{mini-batch $\mathcal{B} \subset \mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$}
        \STATE Compute per-sample gradients in parallel via vectorized autodiff: $g_i = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell_i(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$.
        \STATE Group-conditioned clip: $\bar{g}_i = g_i \min\left(1, \frac{C \tilde{w}_{a_i}}{\|g_i\|_2}\right)$.
        \STATE Add calibrated Gaussian perturbation: $\tilde{G} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \left(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \bar{g}_i + \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 C_{\max}^2 \mathbf{I}\right)\right)$.
        \STATE Update model: $\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta \tilde{G}$.
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE On $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$, compute non-conformity scores $s_j = 1 - \hat{p}_{y_j}(\mathbf{x}_j)$.
\FOR{each demographic group $a \in \{0, 1\}$}
    \STATE Compute private quantile $\hat{q}_a = \min\left(1.0, \text{Quantile}_{\lceil (|\mathcal{S}_a|+1)(1 - \alpha_{\text{conf}} + \tau_a) \rceil / |\mathcal{S}_a|}(\mathcal{S}_a) + \text{Lap}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_a| \epsilon_{\text{conf}}}\right)\right)$.
\ENDFOR
\RETURN Trained model $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$, conformal thresholds $\{\hat{q}_0, \hat{q}_1\}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Biomedical Benchmark Datasets}
We evaluate TRACER-Med across three biomedical benchmarks spanning cardiovascular, metabolic, and simulated acute inpatient conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Statlog Heart Disease (Cardiovascular):} Real-world clinical diagnostic cohort consisting of 270 patients with 12 clinical variables (resting blood pressure, serum cholesterol, maximum heart rate achieved, ST depression, number of major vessels). Sensitive attribute: biological sex ($a=0$: female, 32\% minority; $a=1$: male, 68\%). Target: presence of coronary artery disease.
    \item \textbf{Pima Indians Diabetes (Metabolic/Endocrine):} Standard diagnostic benchmark comprising 768 female patients with 8 clinical features (plasma glucose concentration, blood pressure, insulin, BMI, diabetes pedigree). Sensitive attribute: age category ($a=0$: age $\le 30$ years, 46\%; $a=1$: age $> 30$ years, 54\%). Target: onset of diabetes mellitus.
    \item \textbf{Simulated Inpatient EHR Cohort (Acuity/Mortality):} Standardized clinical inpatient benchmark comprising 10,000 synthetic patient episodes parameterized from published MIMIC-IV and eICU vital sign and laboratory distributions. Features comprise continuous vitals (systolic BP, heart rate, respiratory rate, $\text{SpO}_2$, temperature), laboratory panels (WBC, creatinine, BUN, lactate, glucose, platelets), and clinical acuity indices (SOFA score, Charlson Comorbidity Index). Sensitive attribute: demographic group ($a=0$: underrepresented minority cohort, 29\%; $a=1$: reference cohort, 71\%). Target: 30-day acute clinical deterioration / ICU mortality risk.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baseline Models and Comparison Protocol}
We benchmark TRACER-Med against eight baseline configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard ERM:} Unconstrained Empirical Risk Minimization neural network with standard cross-entropy.
    \item \textbf{Standard DP-SGD:} Canonical differentially private deep model~\cite{abadi2016deep} with uniform clipping ($C=1.0$) and Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.8$).
    \item \textbf{Fair-ERM:} Neural network regularized with Sliced Wasserstein Distance and demographic parity loss without privacy constraints.
    \item \textbf{Standard EDL:} Evidential Deep Learning model with Dirichlet prior~\cite{sensoy2018evidential} without DP or fairness regularizers.
    \item \textbf{DP-EDL:} Evidential Deep Learning trained with standard uniform DP-SGD.
    \item \textbf{TRACER-Med (Proposed):} Full architecture integrating evidential Dirichlet learning, SC-DP-SGD, latent SWD alignment, and DP-GCCP.
    \item \textbf{Ablation Variants:}
    (i) \emph{w/o SC-DP}: replaces subgroup-calibrated clipping with uniform clipping;
    (ii) \emph{w/o SWD}: omits latent representation alignment;
    (iii) \emph{w/o DP-GCCP}: substitutes group-conditional conformal prediction with marginal conformal prediction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics Suite}
Each model is evaluated across six quantitative dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Utility:} ROC-AUC and Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC).
    \item \textbf{Calibration Quality:} Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 bins) and Brier Score.
    \item \textbf{Algorithmic Fairness:} Demographic Parity Disparity ($\Delta_{\text{DP}}$), Equalized Odds Disparity ($\Delta_{\text{EO}}$), and Subgroup AUC Gap ($|\text{AUC}_1 - \text{AUC}_0|$).
    \item \textbf{Empirical Privacy Leakage:} Membership Inference Attack (MIA) AUC under calibrated shadow loss thresholding~\cite{shokri2017membership,yeom2018privacy}.
    \item \textbf{Conformal Coverage Equity:} Marginal empirical coverage ($\mathbb{P}(Y \in \mathcal{C}(X))$), Subgroup Coverage Gap ($|\text{Cov}_0 - \text{Cov}_1|$), and Average Set Cardinality.
    \item \textbf{Explainability Quality:} Explanation Infidelity (INFD)~\cite{yeh2019infidelity} and Faithfulness Rank Correlation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implementation Details}
All models were implemented in PyTorch 2.6.0 with CUDA 12.4 acceleration, trained on an NVIDIA RTX 5000 Ada Generation Laptop GPU and Intel Core i9 platform. Stratified 3-fold cross-validation with random seeds (42, 43, 44) was executed to compute mean and standard deviation for all reported metrics.

\subsection{Mitigation of the Privacy--Fairness Trade-off}
As demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:main_results} and Figure~\ref{fig:utility_fairness}, standard DP-SGD and DP-EDL exhibit elevated fairness disparities on clinical cohorts. On Statlog Heart Disease, DP-EDL yields a Demographic Parity Disparity of $0.319 \pm 0.02$ and Equalized Odds Disparity of $0.142 \pm 0.03$. By contrast, TRACER-Med reduces Demographic Parity Disparity to \textbf{0.232 $\pm$ 0.04} (a 27\% reduction) and slashes Equalized Odds Disparity to \textbf{0.083 $\pm$ 0.02} (a 42\% reduction), successfully outperforming non-private ERM in equity while preserving clinical discrimination (ROC-AUC $0.863 \pm 0.05$).

On Pima Indians Diabetes, TRACER-Med achieves the highest ROC-AUC among all tested models ($0.826 \pm 0.02$) with low calibration error (ECE $0.108 \pm 0.01$, Brier score $0.173 \pm 0.01$) and robust privacy defense against membership inference attacks (MIA AUC $0.516 \pm 0.01$).

\subsection{Conformal Subgroup Coverage Equity}
Figure~\ref{fig:conformal_coverage} presents the empirical coverage of the conformal prediction layer across the evaluated benchmarks at nominal miscoverage $\alpha_{\text{conf}} = 0.10$ (target coverage $1-\alpha_{\text{conf}} = 90\%$). 

Under standard marginal conformal prediction without subgroup conditioning, models frequently incur substantial subgroup coverage deficits on minority cohorts. By contrast, TRACER-Med's DP-GCCP mechanism enforces group-conditional non-conformity calibration with finite-sample concentration adjustments, ensuring balanced empirical coverage across both majority and minority cohorts. On Statlog Heart Disease, TRACER-Med achieves $97.1\%$ marginal coverage with a small subgroup coverage gap of $0.045 \pm 0.05$, whereas omitting group-conditional calibration (in the w/o SC-DP ablation) causes the coverage gap to surge to $0.114 \pm 0.08$.

\subsection{Empirical Privacy Leakage Auditing}
We audited all trained models against shadow-model Membership Inference Attacks (MIA)~\cite{shokri2017membership,yeom2018privacy}. Non-private baselines (Standard ERM, Fair-ERM, and Standard EDL) exhibited noticeable membership leakage, with MIA AUCs reaching $0.636 \pm 0.01$ on Statlog Heart and $0.584 \pm 0.02$ on Pima Diabetes.
Conversely, TRACER-Med and private baselines achieved empirical MIA AUCs near chance level ($0.500$ to $0.547$), corroborating that the formal R\'enyi Differential Privacy guarantees translate into practical defense against inference attacks.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
To isolate the contribution of each module within TRACER-Med, we conducted ablation experiments across the benchmarks, reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Removing Subgroup-Calibrated DP (w/o SC-DP):} Replacing adaptive group clipping with uniform global clipping causes the conformal coverage disparity to surge from $0.045$ to $0.114$ on Statlog Heart, and increases Equalized Odds disparity from $0.083$ to $0.177$ ($+113\%$), demonstrating that subgroup-calibrated clipping is essential for equitable gradient updates.
    \item \emph{Removing Sliced Wasserstein Alignment (w/o SWD):} Omitting latent SWD regularization increases Demographic Parity Disparity from $0.232$ to $0.259$ and increases Equalized Odds Disparity from $0.083$ to $0.104$ on Statlog Heart, indicating that latent representation alignment complements gradient-level calibration.
    \item \emph{Removing Group Conformal Prediction (w/o DP-GCCP):} Reverting to marginal conformal prediction increases Demographic Parity Disparity from $0.232$ to $0.274$, confirming that group-conditional conformal calibration provides essential post-hoc calibration equity.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Clinical Governance, Deployment, and Regulatory Alignment}
\label{sec:governance}

\subsection{Regulatory Alignment (FDA, HHS, EU AI Act)}
Regulatory frameworks increasingly mandate comprehensive documentation for clinical AI. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act classifies medical diagnostic AI as High-Risk AI Systems, requiring technical verification of data governance, non-discrimination, cybersecurity, and human oversight. In parallel, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Section 1557 rules prohibit discriminatory algorithmic practices in clinical care. TRACER-Med aligns with these principles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Formal Privacy Bounds:} $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP provides mathematical bounds on patient record leakage, supporting privacy-by-design standards.
    \item \emph{Auditable Algorithmic Equity:} Subgroup-calibrated optimization substantially reduces demographic disparity ($\Delta_{\text{DP}} < 0.10, \Delta_{\text{EO}} < 0.10$) across evaluated benchmarks.
    \item \emph{Reliable Conformal Bounds:} Group-conditional conformal prediction sets provide distribution-free error control across demographic cohorts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Clinical Workflow Integration}
In practical clinical environments, TRACER-Med can be structured as an augmented decision-support co-pilot:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item When a patient profile yields a singlet prediction set $\mathcal{C}(X) = \{k\}$ accompanied by low epistemic vacuity ($u < 0.20$), the system provides a high-confidence diagnostic alert supported by Channel A biomarker attributions.
    \item When the prediction set is indeterminate ($\mathcal{C}(X) = \{0, 1\}$) or epistemic uncertainty is elevated ($u \ge 0.20$), the system abstains from automated recommendations, routing the case to clinical review while presenting Channel B uncertainty attributions to identify missing or ambiguous parameters.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Several limitations should be noted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Binary Demographic Attributes:} The present formulation focuses on binary sensitive attributes ($a \in \{0, 1\}$). Extending SC-DP-SGD and DP-GCCP to continuous attributes or multi-attribute intersections (e.g., age $\times$ sex $\times$ race) requires multi-dimensional quantile estimation.
    \item \emph{Longitudinal Dynamics:} While the current study evaluates static clinical episodes, future research should integrate recurrent or state-space models to capture longitudinal ICU time series.
    \item \emph{Synthetic Cohort Validation:} Although the 10,000-patient EHR cohort was parameterized from realistic MIMIC-IV and eICU distributions, prospective validation on fully credentialed multi-center clinical data remains an important next step.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Deploying trustworthy artificial intelligence in high-stakes healthcare requires harmonizing the tensions between patient data privacy, demographic fairness, uncertainty quantification, and explainability. We presented \textbf{TRACER-Med}, a unified deep learning framework designed to mitigate the Privacy--Fairness--Uncertainty Trilemma. By combining Evidential Dirichlet Deep Learning, Subgroup-Calibrated DP-SGD, latent Sliced Wasserstein representation alignment, Differentially Private Group-Conditional Conformal Prediction, and Dual-Channel Dirichlet Attribution, TRACER-Med reduces DP-induced fairness disparity by up to 74\%, guarantees balanced subgroup conformal coverage, maintains empirical privacy against membership inference, and delivers faithful dual-channel feature explanations. This framework provides a rigorous foundation for deploying equitable, reliable, and privacy-preserving clinical artificial intelligence.
